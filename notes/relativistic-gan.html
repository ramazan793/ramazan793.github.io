Why is the relativistic GAN loss better?

November 2026

R3GAN (Huang et al., 2024) is built on the relativistic GAN loss instead of the standard one, and the reason given is mode dropping. The argument goes back to Sun et al. (2020): the standard loss has many bad local minima, which correspond to mode collapse, and the relativistic loss has none. Let's try to understand why the relativistic GAN is better.

First, recall the standard GAN loss:

$$\min_G \max_D \;\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log \sigma(D(x))\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\big[\log \sigma(-D(G(z)))\big]$$

Here $D(x)$ is the logit of the discriminator and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid, so a high $D$ means "real". Every sample is scored on its own, against the same threshold $D = 0$.

Then, what Sun et al. proposed is to score a real sample and a fake sample against each other:

$$\min_G \max_D \;\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}},\; z \sim p_z}\big[\log \sigma\big(D(x) - D(G(z))\big)\big]$$

This is the relativistic pairing GAN, RpGAN. The same loss had been introduced earlier by Jolicoeur-Martineau (2018). There is no threshold any more. $D$ only has to say which sample of the pair looks more real.

A simple example

Let's now take a look at a simple example of 2 samples. The dataset is two points, $x_1$ and $x_2$. The generator also produces two points, $y_1$ and $y_2$. In the relativistic loss $y_1$ is paired with $x_1$ and $y_2$ with $x_2$, as in Sun et al. The two losses are then

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}} = \tfrac{1}{4}\Big[&\log\sigma(D(x_1)) + \log\sigma(D(x_2)) \\ +\;&\log\sigma(-D(y_1)) + \log\sigma(-D(y_2))\Big] \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{RpGAN}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[&\log\sigma\big(D(x_1) - D(y_1)\big) \\ +\;&\log\sigma\big(D(x_2) - D(y_2)\big)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

Suppose the generator has collapsed: both $y_1$ and $y_2$ sit on $x_1$, and nothing covers $x_2$. To get out, $y_2$ has to leave $x_1$ and travel to $x_2$. What happens to the loss on the way?

There is only one loss $\mathcal{L}$, and it is the same number for both players: $D$ maximizes it, and the generator minimizes it. So for every position of the fake samples we first let $D$ maximize, and then look at the value that is left:

$$\mathcal{L}^* = \max_D \mathcal{L}$$

This is the loss that the generator sees. The generator moves the fake samples to make $\mathcal{L}^*$ lower. If $D$ can be any function at all, $\mathcal{L}^*$ takes only three values:

Standard GAN. While $y_2$ sits on $x_1$, there are one real and two fake samples at the same point, so $D$ has to give all three the same probability of being real, $p = \sigma(D(x_1))$. The real one adds $\log p$ to the loss and each fake adds $\log(1 - p)$:

$$\max_p \; \big[\log p + 2\log(1 - p)\big] \quad\Rightarrow\quad p = \tfrac{1}{3}$$

$x_2$ is alone, so $D$ can overfit to it: $\sigma(D(x_2)) = 1$, and its term is $\log 1 = 0$. Once $y_2$ steps away from $x_1$, to anywhere else, it is alone too. $D$ calls it fake at no cost, and its term is also $\log 1$. One real and one fake are left on $x_1$, where now $D$ is such that $p = 1/2$, because $D$ is optimal.

$$\begin{aligned} y_2 \text{ on } x_1: &\quad \tfrac{1}{4}\Big[\underbrace{\log\tfrac{1}{3} + 2\log\tfrac{2}{3}}_{x_1,\; y_1,\; y_2} + \underbrace{\log 1}_{x_2}\Big] \approx -0.48 \\[8pt] y_2 \text{ elsewhere}: &\quad \tfrac{1}{4}\Big[\underbrace{2\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{x_1,\; y_1} + \underbrace{\log 1}_{x_2} + \underbrace{\log 1}_{y_2}\Big] \approx -0.35 \\[8pt] y_2 \text{ on } x_2: &\quad \tfrac{1}{4}\Big[\underbrace{2\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{x_1,\; y_1} + \underbrace{2\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{x_2,\; y_2}\Big] \approx -0.69 \end{aligned}$$

The generator minimizes $\mathcal{L}^*$, so the first step away from $x_1$ is uphill, and $\mathcal{L}^*$ stays up until $y_2$ lands exactly on $x_2$. Mode collapse is a local minimum. On $x_1$ the fake hides behind a real sample, and anywhere else it is exposed. And nothing pulls it toward $x_2$: the term $\log\sigma(D(x_2))$ does not depend on the generator at all.

RpGAN. In the pair $(x_1, y_1)$ both points are at the same place, so $D$ gives them the same value, $D(x_1) = D(y_1)$, and the term of this pair is

$$\log\sigma\big(D(x_1) - D(y_1)\big) = \log\sigma(0) = \log\tfrac{1}{2}$$

In the pair $(x_2, y_2)$ the points are at different places until $y_2$ reaches $x_2$. So $D$ can make $D(x_2)$ much larger than $D(y_2)$, and the term of this pair goes to $\log\sigma(+\infty) = \log 1 = 0$.

$$\begin{aligned} y_2 \text{ on } x_1: &\quad \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\underbrace{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{(x_1,\, y_1)} + \underbrace{\log 1}_{(x_2,\, y_2)}\Big] \approx -0.35 \\[8pt] y_2 \text{ elsewhere}: &\quad \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\underbrace{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{(x_1,\, y_1)} + \underbrace{\log 1}_{(x_2,\, y_2)}\Big] \approx -0.35 \\[8pt] y_2 \text{ on } x_2: &\quad \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\underbrace{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{(x_1,\, y_1)} + \underbrace{\log\tfrac{1}{2}}_{(x_2,\, y_2)}\Big] \approx -0.69 \end{aligned}$$

Leaving $x_1$ costs nothing, so collapse is not a local minimum any more. $y_2$ is never compared with $x_1$, so it gains nothing from sitting on it. It is only compared with $x_2$, the sample it is supposed to cover.

A smooth discriminator

In the table the standard loss has a hill between collapse and the solution. The relativistic loss has a plateau instead: $\mathcal{L}^*$ is $-0.35$ wherever $y_2$ is, and it drops to $-0.69$ only when $y_2$ is exactly on $x_2$. A plateau has no hill, but it has no gradient either, so it does not tell the generator where to go.

The plateau exists only because $D$ could be any function. Such a $D$ keeps the difference $D(x_2) - D(y_2)$ huge even when $y_2$ is very close to $x_2$. A real discriminator is a smooth function and cannot do that: when $y_2$ comes close to $x_2$, $D(y_2)$ comes close to $D(x_2)$. So the difference has to shrink on the way, and the term of the pair $(x_2, y_2)$

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\log\sigma\big(D(x_2) - D(y_2)\big)$$

goes from $\tfrac{1}{2}\log 1$ down to $\tfrac{1}{2}\log\tfrac{1}{2}$ gradually, not in one jump at the very end. The plateau turns into a slope toward $x_2$. The slope never goes the wrong way, because this term depends only on the distance between $y_2$ and $x_2$.

For the standard loss, smoothness changes nothing important. When $y_2$ leaves $x_1$, $D$ can separate it from $x_1$ better and better, so $\mathcal{L}^*$ still goes up first. The hill stays.

In the bottom plot the relativistic loss goes down all the way from $x_1$ to $x_2$. The standard loss first goes up, until $y_2$ is about 38% of the way.

One thing to notice: far from $x_2$ the relativistic loss is still almost flat, and it gets steep only near $x_2$. A smooth $D$ makes the gradient point the right way, but it does not make it large. How far the slope reaches depends on how smooth $D$ is.

Details of the figure. The points live on a line with $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = 1$, $y_1$ stays on $x_1$, and the discriminator is

$$D(x) = b + \sum_j \alpha_j \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x - p_j)^2}{2 s^2}\Big)$$

with one bump on every occupied point $p_j$ and $s = 0.3$. For each position of $y_2$ it is trained to its optimum with Newton's method, with a small penalty $0.003\,\lVert D\rVert^2$ that keeps it smooth. The bottom plot is $\mathcal{L}^*$, the loss at that optimum. The arrow is $-\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_2$ with $D$ fixed, which at the optimal $D$ is minus the slope of the bottom plot. Its length is on a log scale.
